How Much Does a New Roof Cost in Cincinnati in 2026?

By Joe Deal · No Big Deal Home Solutions · Greater Cincinnati, OH · Updated September 2026

Short answer: in 2026, most full roof replacements in Greater Cincinnati run $14,800 to $20,600 for a typical 20–25 square house with the shingle most of my customers pick: GAF Timberline HDZ, $740–$825 per roofing square installed. Across my three tiers it’s $615–$965 per square — small, simple roofs start near $9,000; big, steep, premium ones pass $30,000.

Updated September 2026. Every number here comes from the same per-square prices my Instant Estimate tool uses and my homepage publishes — no national averages dressed up as local ones. Below: what a square is, cost by roof size, what each tier gets you, what pushes a price up, and how insurance and financing change what you pay.

What does a new roof cost per square in Cincinnati?

Roofers price by the square: 100 square feet of roof surface. Not the floor space of your house and not its footprint — the actual shingled area, every slope added up. A roof that measures 2,000 square feet is 20 squares.

Here’s what I charge per square, installed, in 2026:

  • Standard (GAF Timberline NS): $615–$690 per square
  • Preferred (GAF Timberline HDZ): $740–$825 per square — where about half my customers land
  • Elite (GAF Timberline UHDZ + The NBD Build): $860–$965 per square

Why a range instead of one number? Mostly pitch. A steep roof eats more material per square — more cuts, more waste — and takes longer to work safely. The low end of each range is a low-slope, walkable roof; the high end is a steep one. That’s already built into the per-square number, so I don’t tack a separate “waste” charge on top of it.

What the per-square price includes

  • Tear-off of one layer of old shingles, down to the deck, with the dumpster and haul-away
  • Fresh underlayment, plus ice-and-water shield where the roof needs it (eaves and valleys)
  • New drip edge and flashing, and ridge ventilation (GAF Cobra on every tier)
  • The shingles, starter and ridge cap for your tier, and the labor to install them
  • Cleanup, and The Pledge — call me about anything I worked on, for as long as I’m in business and you own the house, and I’ll come look for free

What it doesn’t include: material delivery, which is a flat $150 per job; rotten decking nobody can see until the old roof is off; extra layers of old shingles; and unusual details like skylights, chimney work or low-slope sections. Those are separate lines on a written estimate. I’ll get to each of them below.

How many squares is my roof?

Honest answer: nobody knows until the roof is measured, because roof area isn’t living area. For a first guess, my Instant Estimate starts from rough benchmarks: a smaller home around 1,200 square feet is often in the neighborhood of 14 squares, a typical 2,000-square-foot home around 20, and a large 3,000-square-foot home around 30.

Real houses throw that off both ways. A one-story ranch puts all its living space under one roof, so it carries more roof per square foot of house than a two-story the same size. Overhangs, an attached garage, a porch roof and a steep pitch all add squares. Treat the house-size guess as a starting point and the measurement as the answer.

What makes one roof cost more than another?

Pitch. Already in the per-square range, but it’s the biggest reason two houses with the same square count get different quotes. A walkable 4/12 ranch and a 10/12 roof that needs roof jacks and harnesses on every run are different jobs.

Layers. The table assumes one layer. Two or three layers mean more tear-off labor, more weight in the dumpster and more time — and in older neighborhoods I find them a lot. Each extra layer is priced on your estimate, in writing before we start — not after the dumpster fills up.

Decking. Rotten or soft boards only show up once the old shingles are off, and a lot of older homes around here still have the original one-by plank decking under them. The honest way to handle it is a per-sheet price for replacement decking, priced on your estimate before the job, so replacing a few sheets is simple arithmetic instead of a surprise halfway through.

Ventilation. Ridge vent is included on every tier. But a ridge vent needs intake air coming in low — usually soffit vents — and if the attic doesn’t have enough, or has mismatched vents working against each other, fixing that is extra. It’s often worth it: a hot attic cooks shingles from underneath.

Flashing and details. Chimneys, skylights, dormers, and every spot where the roof meets a wall are hand work. Re-flashing a wide chimney with a new cricket behind it takes a lot more time than shingling a plain slope — and it’s where roofs leak, so it’s the wrong place to save money.

Complexity. A simple two-slope gable goes fast. A roof with seven facets, hips and valleys means more cuts and more hours per square, even at the same size.

Permits. In Ohio, the Board of Building Standards put out a memo in 2017 on approvals and inspections for re-roofing 1-, 2- and 3-family homes: approval goes through the certified building department that has jurisdiction where you live, and the state residential code (OAC 4101:8) is what it enforces. Whether your city or township requires a permit, and what it charges, varies. In Kentucky it comes down to local ordinance. When a permit is required, it’s its own line on the estimate — never a surprise afterward.

Does a new roof cost more in some Cincinnati suburbs?

Not really, and I’d be suspicious of any page that lists a different roof price for every suburb. I price the roof, not the ZIP code — Mason, Anderson Township, Covington and Florence get the same per-square pricing. What changes from town to town is the housing: older river-town and city neighborhoods tend to have steeper roofs, plank decking and more old layers; newer subdivisions tend to have bigger roofs with more facets; and every jurisdiction runs its own permit process. That changes the price because it changes the roof, not because of the address.

Should I pay cash or go through insurance?

It depends on why the roof needs replacing.

If it’s just old — curling, bare of granules, twenty-some years of Ohio weather — that’s a retail job. Homeowners insurance covers sudden damage from a covered peril, not wear and tear, and anyone who tells you otherwise is setting you up for a denied claim.

If a storm damaged it — hail or wind — it may be an insurance claim. Who to call first depends: with an active leak, get it tarped and report the damage promptly; otherwise, an inspection first helps you decide whether to file at all. Either way, report promptly; most policies give about a year — check yours. On an approved claim, your out-of-pocket looks very different:

  • Deductible. It’s yours to pay on any approved claim. Check whether yours is a flat dollar amount or a percentage of your dwelling coverage — plenty of homeowners find out at claim time.
  • RCV (replacement cost value). The policy pays what it takes to replace the roof, minus your deductible. Carriers commonly pay the depreciated amount first and release the rest once the work is done.
  • ACV (actual cash value). The policy pays the depreciated value of your old roof. On an older roof that can be a fraction of replacement, and the gap is yours.

My part on a storm job: once you’ve filed, I inspect and document the damage with photos, write the line-item estimate, meet the adjuster on the roof, and send a supplement — my updated estimate — if the adjuster’s scope missed something. The claim stays yours; you file it and you make the calls on it. The details are in does homeowners insurance cover hail damage in Ohio and on my hail damage claims page.

One thing I won’t do: waive, rebate or “eat” your deductible. In Kentucky that’s illegal for a contractor under KRS 367.628. In Ohio, a deductible that quietly disappears usually means the estimate sent to your insurer was padded to cover it — that’s insurance fraud risk, and it’s your name on the claim. If a roofer offers it, that tells you how they do everything else. If the deductible is the hard part, financing it is legal and above board.

Can I finance a new roof?

Yes. I offer financing through Acorn Finance, a home improvement lending marketplace: one short application, and lenders in their network come back with real offers — rate, term and monthly payment. Checking your options is a soft credit pull that doesn’t touch your score; a hard inquiry only happens if you pick a specific lender’s offer and finish that lender’s full application. Rates and terms depend on your credit and the lender, so I’m not going to quote you a monthly payment on a blog post.

Financing generally makes the most sense for projects of $3,000 and up, and it can cover an insurance deductible or the part of an insurance job that isn’t covered. If it isn’t the right move for you, I’ll say so. More in how roof financing actually works.

What if my roof only needs a repair?

Then you shouldn’t be pricing a replacement. Repairs are priced by the job, not by the square. One recent example: a damaged gutter section replaced and storm-loosened shingles repaired in Cincinnati came in at $500–$800. If a repair genuinely solves your problem, that’s what I’ll quote — see roof repair and signs your roof needs replacement vs. repair.

Roof cost questions homeowners ask

How much does a new roof cost in Cincinnati in 2026?

Most full replacements in Greater Cincinnati run $14,800 to $20,600 for a typical 20–25 square house on the Preferred tier (GAF Timberline HDZ, $740–$825 per square installed). Across all three NBD tiers the range is $615–$965 per square, so a small, simple roof starts near $9,000 and a large, steep, premium roof can pass $30,000.

What is the average cost per square for roofing in Cincinnati?

NBD’s 2026 installed prices are $615–$690 per square for Standard (GAF Timberline NS), $740–$825 for Preferred (GAF Timberline HDZ) and $860–$965 for Elite (GAF Timberline UHDZ). A square is 100 square feet of roof surface. The low end of each range is a low-slope roof and the high end a steep one; one layer of tear-off is included.

How many squares is a typical house roof?

As a rough starting point, a home around 1,200 square feet is often near 14 squares, a 2,000-square-foot home near 20, and a 3,000-square-foot home near 30. Roof area isn’t living area, though — ranches, overhangs, garages and steep pitch add squares, so only a measurement gives the real count.

How much more do the Preferred and Elite tiers cost?

Preferred runs about 20% more than Standard and Elite about 40% more on a typical home. On a 25-square roof that’s roughly $3,100–$3,300 more for Preferred and $6,100–$6,800 more for Elite.

Does insurance cover roof replacement in Ohio?

If a covered peril like hail or wind damaged the roof, most Ohio homeowners policies pay for replacement minus your deductible — the full replacement cost on an RCV policy, or the depreciated value on an ACV policy. Age and wear and tear are not covered.

Can a roofer pay or waive my insurance deductible?

No. In Kentucky it is illegal for a contractor to waive, rebate or absorb your deductible (KRS 367.628), and in Ohio covering it usually means padding the estimate sent to your insurer, which is insurance fraud risk. The deductible is yours to pay; financing it is legal.

How long does a roof replacement take in Cincinnati?

Most homes take 1 day. Large or complex roofs, with multiple valleys, dormers or a steep pitch, take 2 days. The full timeline is in how long a roof replacement takes.
