How a Roof Actually Works

A roof isn’t just shingles. It’s a stack of parts that each hand water to the next one down. These drawings show every layer, every lap and every piece of flashing, the way the manufacturers and the building code call for it.

By Joe Deal, owner of No Big Deal Home Solutions · October 2026

The whole roof, pulled apart

Drag the slider to put the roof together or pull it apart, and tap any layer to see what it does. Each layer has one job, and they go on in this order.

The edges: eave, rake, starter

Most of the water on a roof leaves over the bottom edge, the eave. The order of the layers there is the difference between water dripping into the gutter and water running behind the fascia.

Up the sloped side edges, the rakes, the drip edge and the underlayment swap places.

The field: nailing and impact rating

Shingles hold on by four small nails each. Where those nails go matters more than how many there are.

“Class 4” gets thrown around a lot after hail. Here’s what the rating actually measures. The GAF Timberline UHDZ I install is UL 2218 Class 4.

Where leaks start: walls, valleys, pipes

Shingles rarely leak in the middle of a slope. Leaks start where the roof meets something: a wall, another slope, a pipe or a chimney. That’s where the flashing does the work.

Air and ice: ventilation and ice dams

An attic has to breathe: fresh air in low at the soffits, warm damp air out high at the ridge. The arrows below show the path.

In a Cincinnati winter, a warm attic and a cold overhang are how ice dams start.

Walls, windows and gutters

Siding is the part you see. What keeps the wall dry is behind it: the housewrap, the tape and the flashing, every piece lapped so water runs out.
