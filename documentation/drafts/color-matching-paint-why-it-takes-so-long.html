Why paint color matching
takes so long.

By Joe Deal October 2026 4 min read

A good match takes about ten minutes. A hard one can take forty-five. Here is what is going on behind the counter, and what to bring so you only make the trip once.

I have stood in a lot of paint counter lines. Enough that it feels like half my life. Contractors do it, homeowners do it, and the question is always the same: why is the person ahead of me still there?

Here is the short version. A good match takes me about ten minutes. A bad one can run thirty to forty-five. Same counter, same machine. The difference is almost always the sample.

Why One Match Takes Ten Minutes and Another Takes Forty-Five

The machine at the counter reads the color of whatever you hand it, then gives the person mixing a starting formula. That part is fast. The slow part comes after: mixing a small amount, letting it dry, checking it against what you brought, and adjusting. If it is close, you are done. If it is a little off, they go around again.

The hard ones are the ones where the sample fights the machine. Old, faded paint. A rough or textured surface. A glossy finish. A chip so small it barely covers the reading window. A spot that is dirty or chalky. All of those make the first reading less accurate, so there are more rounds of mixing and checking.

"Take a sample, no matter how small. The spectrometer is amazing. It is just slow work, and hard to get perfect." Joe

That is the thing I tell everybody. A tiny sample beats a phone photo every time, because a screen is not a color sample. Your phone changes the color, the light in the room changes it again, and nobody at the counter can read it.

How to Skip a Second Trip

  • Bring the real thing. A paint chip, a small piece of trim, or a chip of the wall. Bigger than a quarter is better, but small is still better than nothing.
  • Pick a clean, flat spot. Wipe off dirt and chalk first. Avoid a corner that took extra sun or got scuffed.
  • Say the finish. Color and sheen are two different choices. Tell them if it was flat, satin, semi-gloss, or gloss, and whether it is for inside or outside.
  • Test before you buy the rest. Paint a small patch and let it dry. Wet paint looks different, so do not judge it until it is dry.
  • Buy enough for the whole job. Two cans mixed on two days can come out a hair different. Ask them to mix what you need together, and keep the formula label for next time.

Expect that "close" is a win. Matching paint that has been on a wall for years is hard to get perfect, and anyone who says otherwise has not done many of them. If a patch is going to show, plan to paint the whole wall or the whole side so the edges never matter.
